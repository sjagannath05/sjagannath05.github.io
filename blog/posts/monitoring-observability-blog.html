Federating another Prometheus: honor_labels and a timeout that would not load

One scrape job, three commits in an afternoon, and the two settings that decide whether federated samples keep their own identity.

Monitoring

One scrape job, three commits in an afternoon, and the two settings that decide whether federated samples keep their own identity.

The job

Partner D ran its own Prometheus collectors. The agent-based collection alongside them was meant to be retired once the dashboards and alerts moved across. The replacement is a plain /federate scrape against four collector addresses (port 9090, static targets, no service discovery), added to one production cluster's scrape config:

- job_name: 'partner-d_federate'
  honor_labels: true
  metrics_path: '/federate'
  scrape_interval: 1m
  scrape_timeout: 1m
  params:
    'match[]':
      - '{__name__=~".+"}'
  static_configs:
    - targets:
        - 172.22.144.22:9090
        - 172.22.8.103:9090
        - 172.22.16.103:9090
        - 172.22.48.10:9090
  relabel_configs:
    - source_labels: [job]
      target_label: secondary_job
    - target_label: job
      replacement: partner-d_federate
    - source_labels: [instance]
      target_label: secondary_instance
    - target_label: instance
      replacement: partner-d_federate_instance

Nothing there is clever. The three lines that changed after the first commit are the whole story.

The timeout that would not load

It went in as scrape_interval: 30s with scrape_timeout: 2m — a pair Prometheus refuses, because a timeout longer than its own interval fails validation at parse time. The failure is the whole file (every job in it), not the one job that is wrong. A second commit the same afternoon pulled the timeout back to 30s; a third moved both to 1m.

A match[] of {__name__=~".+"} asks each collector for everything it holds (every series, every job) — no name filter at all. The parent then carries a full copy of four collectors' series every cycle, and 30 seconds is a thin budget for that. One minute (both interval and timeout) is the number that stayed.

What honor_labels actually decides

The default (false) means the target's labels win a collision. A scraped sample carrying its own job and instance has them renamed to exported_job and exported_instance, and the target's values are written in their place. For an ordinary exporter that is correct; the exporter has no opinion about which job it belongs to. For a federate scrape it destroys the thing being federated — every series pulled from all four collectors arrives wearing job="partner-d_federate", and the real job survives only under a prefix (exported_) that no existing dashboard query mentions.

Setting it to true reverses the precedence. The sample's own labels win, the colliding target labels are dropped, and a federated series arrives looking the way it looked on the collector that produced it. The commit subject says what it is for in four words: proper label replacement.

The half that misses

The relabel_configs were meant to park the target identity under names that cannot collide, so you could still tell which of the four collectors answered. secondary_job carries a value, though a useless one (the job name, which the next rule was about to set to the same string). secondary_instance carries nothing — instance is absent from a target during relabelling (it is filled in from __address__ afterwards), so source_labels: [instance] reads an empty string, and an empty label is dropped. Reading __address__ is the way to carry a collector address through; this job reads the wrong one.

What it cost

Three commits on 30 April 2025 in one pull request, plus a one-line target addition the previous December (one address, one line) and a separate single-target job in July (one VM, port 19100). The repository was marked deprecated in May 2026 and every production overlay removed (nine removal commits in a single day), so the job outlived neither the plan it belonged to nor the repository it lived in.
